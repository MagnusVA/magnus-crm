The lead lifecycle: what we built, what it costs, and what to change

0The short answer

Your read is right. The CRM has no record of a person until a Slack qualifier or a Calendly booking creates one. Lead gen and DM work happen outside the lead. One opportunity status field carries four different jobs. A customer is a second copy of the lead.

Whether it scales depends on what you mean by scale. At runtime it holds up. One tenant has about 10,800 opportunities and 19,000 identifiers, and the reads are bounded, the counts come from aggregates, and the webhook pipeline is durable. As a model it does not scale. Slack setters, lead gen and DM closers each arrived with their own person table, staff table, schedule table, attribution fields and projections. The next role will repeat the pattern. And the 68 people merged into 4 leads are the predictable result of deciding who someone is at booking time, with heuristics, instead of at first contact.

I would not rewrite it. Make the lead (I will call it the person) the first record in the funnel, and give each concern its own field. Lifecycle stage goes on the person. Stage and outcome go on the deal. Attendance goes on the meeting. Due dates go on tasks. Attribution goes to team members through one table. Section 7 splits this into seven phases, 0 to 6, revised after your decisions in section 9. Each phase ships on its own, using the widen, migrate, narrow pattern the repo already follows.

This answer describes the CRM on 14 October. By 20 October Phases 0 to 2 were done: a lead gen capture creates or finds the person at the Prospect stage, Slack qualifications and DM portal notes and edits are touches on that person, and a Calendly booking finds the person by the identifiers the invitee enters. Section 7 has each phase's status.

3Findings, ranked

Severity reflects how much each problem costs you now and how much worse it gets with each new role or tenant.

F1The person record starts in the middle of the funnel High

What happens. Only resolveLeadIdentity inserts into leads, and its three callers are Slack, Calendly and side deals. Lead gen writes prospects with no lead link. The DM portal cannot create a lead at all.

Why it hurts. "Of the prospects this worker captured in September, how many paid?" has no direct answer. You would have to go through the audit-match table, which only exists for Slack-qualified Instagram handles with an exact match. Lead gen and setter performance end at the handoff, so you cannot measure the full prospect-to-cash conversion that this business is built on.

F3Identity is decided late, by heuristics, and mistakes spread High

What happens. The CRM first decides who someone is when a booking arrives, using whatever the booker typed. Before commit 12e6a6e, placeholder values such as Calendly's sample phone counted as identity keys. Then the 14-day reschedule heuristic attached other people's bookings to earlier canceled or no-show opportunities. In production, 4 leads hold 68 people, 43 opportunities and 85 meetings. One customer sits on a lead whose email belongs to someone else. Another 22 leads look like two people each.

Why it hurts. Undoing it means repointing about 10 lead-keyed tables per person and calling each side-effect helper by hand. Two placeholder classes (fake phones and placeholder emails) are still live. The 14-day heuristic still runs before the Slack check, so a fresh qualification can lose to an old no-show.

F4A customer is a copy of the lead High

What happens. Conversion copies name, email, phone and handles into customers. Only three code paths re-sync them, so a handle added later or the Slack country never reaches the copy. Conversion requires an email, so a Slack-only lead cannot become a customer, and the payment rolls back. Each lead gets one customer with one program and one winning opportunity, so a second purchase never shows on it. churned and paused live on the copy and apply to the whole person rather than to one program.

Why it hurts. This is the Salesforce lead-conversion problem, which Salesforce itself now steers people away from (section 5). It also breaks the most valuable number in a coaching business, revenue per customer across programs.

F5The people who do the work live in four tables Medium

What happens. Closers and admins are users. Lead gen workers are leadGenWorkers, linked to users. Slack qualifiers exist only as a slackUserId, with an optional link to a user. DM closers are dmClosers behind a shared-password portal, with an optional link to a user. Three schedule tables have the same shape. Credit is stored four ways: a qualifiedBy object, dmCloserId, workerId and assignedCloserId.

Why it hurts. One human who prospects in the morning and sets in the afternoon is two unrelated identities. A commission report across roles needs four joins and four identity rules. The next role will add a fifth table. DM credit is also self-asserted: anyone with the shared portal password picks their own DM closer identity, and booking credit comes from the utm_medium string in the link.

F6Attribution is copied, and screens read different copies Medium

What happens. Every meeting stores its own UTMs, DM team and DM closer. The opportunity keeps the first external booking's values. Qualification rows copy the opportunity's values, and the meeting projections copy the meeting's. That makes six copies each of attributionTeamId and dmCloserId. Booked Calls reads the meeting copy, and the qualification reports read the opportunity copy. A Slack opportunity gets its attribution from the first booking that joins it.

Why it hurts. Two dashboards can credit the same deal to different DM teams, and nothing raises an error. Commission questions turn into data archaeology.

F7Side effects are maintained by hand Medium

What happens. The repo has no triggers. A write to a meeting, opportunity, payment, lead or customer must also call the aggregate hooks, tenant counters, domain events, meeting refs and projection rebuilds. docs/agents/convex.md warns that "a missed call leaves dashboards and counts wrong without any error." No single function owns opportunity transitions. patchOpportunityLifecycle covers aggregates and search rows, but it does not validate the transition, update tenant stats, emit the domain event or rebuild qualification rows. It has 25 call sites in 13 files, and 15 raw db.patch calls sit beside them.

What it costs. Marking one meeting lost makes about 20 writes. Logging a payment rebuilds the lead/customer search row up to four times in one transaction, at about 300 reads each. Opportunity status exists in 7 places and the program name in 9, and renaming a program updates only 2 of the 9.

It has probably already gone wrong once. Qualification rows are rebuilt on bookings and on sold-program changes, never on closer outcomes. In logPayment the rebuild runs before the status patch. So the qualifications queue most likely filters on an opportunityStatus that is out of date for anything lost, no-showed or followed up after booking. A production read can confirm it.

Why it hurts. The cost of each new feature grows with the number of projections, and correctness rests on reviewers remembering the list. convex-helpers is installed and ships triggers, but nothing uses them.

F11Runtime hot spots to fix before volume doubles Medium

What happens. These are the places where the runtime verdict "holds up" stops holding.

  • tenantStats is one document per tenant with 39 write sites, including the booking pipeline that runs four jobs in parallel. Every admin dashboard subscribes to it, so concurrent writes conflict and each write re-runs every subscribed query.

4What is worth keeping

The criticism above is about the domain model. A lot of the engineering underneath is solid, and the redesign should build on it.

Multi-identifier identity

leadIdentifiers with type, normalized value, source, confidence and source meeting is the right base, close to how Close and HubSpot handle it. The fix is to use it earlier and with stricter rules, not to replace it.

Durable ingestion

Signed webhooks, permanent delivery receipts, Workpool jobs, idempotent upserts, blocked-delivery recovery. This is better than most CRMs at this stage.

A domain event log

domainEvents already records status changes with actor, source, from and to. It is most of a person timeline once every event carries a person ID.

Explicit state machines

statusTransitions.ts puts the allowed moves in one place. The problem is what the states mean and the code paths that skip the check, not the idea.

Tenant isolation and auth

Tenant, user and role come from the identity, never from arguments, and server-only logic is internal. That carries over unchanged.

Migration discipline

Widen, migrate, narrow with paired assert migrations, plus the split plan's audit-and-undo approach. That discipline is what makes an incremental redesign safe.

The UI has also moved in the right direction already. /leads, /customers, /opportunities and /pipeline now redirect to /workspace/leads-customers, which reads one projection with a lifecycle field. The product already treats lead and customer as one person, and the data model has not caught up.

What each one teaches

HubSpot is the closest match for the person side. The Contact is permanent. Its lifecycle stage runs Subscriber, Lead, MQL, SQL, Opportunity, Customer, Evangelist, and automation only moves it forward. To go back, someone has to clear the value first. Two sync settings move the contact to Opportunity when its first deal is created and to Customer when a deal closes won. A returning customer stays Customer when a new deal opens. Since 2024 a separate Leads object tracks qualification attempts. A lead cannot exist without a contact, one contact can have several, and disqualifying one requires a reason. Deals record when they entered and left each stage, which is what makes funnel timing reports cheap.

Salesforce is mostly a warning. A Lead is a separate record that gets converted into a Contact and Account. After conversion the lead is hidden from most users, and the conversion cannot be undone. Consultants point out that one person ends up in two pools, and that a returning customer comes back as a new Lead that duplicates their Contact. Many teams now run "contacts only". That is our customer-copy problem. The part worth copying is credit. Opportunity Splits hold revenue credit that must total 100%, plus overlay credit for supporting reps that may exceed it. That maps directly onto setter and closer commission.

Close is popular with coaching sales teams. The Lead is the person, before and after the sale, and owns its opportunities. Every opportunity status belongs to a pipeline and has a type of active, won or lost that drives revenue reports. Status changes are stored as activities. Close's coaching playbook does what we do: it creates the opportunity when the call is booked and uses a No-Show status that starts a rebooking workflow. That works while one opportunity has one meeting. Once reschedules give an opportunity several meetings it stops working, and Close has since added per-meeting outcomes.

Pipedrive has the cleanest split between stage and result. A deal has a stage and, separately, a status of open, won, lost or deleted. A lost reason can only be set when the status is lost. Its Leads Inbox keeps unqualified people out of the pipeline, so they don't distort pipeline reports. It also locks the original source so nobody can overwrite it.

GoHighLevel shows the full three-way split. The contact has a type, the opportunity has a custom stage plus a fixed status of open, won, lost or abandoned, and the appointment has its own status (confirmed, showed, no-show, cancelled). Workflows connect them. Contacts keep a first attribution that never changes and a latest attribution that does. The weak spot is that agencies often copy appointment status back into stages, which brings the clutter back.

Setter and closer tools treat each call as having its own outcome. iClosed separates the setter's discovery call (approved or rejected) from the closer's strategy call (sale or no sale, with reasons such as no-show, follow-up scheduled or unqualified). A sale creates a deal with value, product and payment type, either paid in full or a deposit. Hyros tracks each call as qualified, unqualified, canceled or no-show, and argues that booked, showed and closed are three separate events whose rates differ by ad source. Dialers such as Aloware keep a disposition per call and a separate disposition for the contact. One practitioner's sales scorecard pays setters per call that shows and per close, not per booking. An Instagram DM tool puts the reason bluntly: paid per booking, "setters will book anyone who breathes" (DM Tracker, vendor content).

The pattern across all of them

  • One person record for life. Only Salesforce converts and copies, and that is the part of it people work around.
  • Won and lost are a status, or a type attached to each stage, never just one more stage name.
  • The more specialised the tool is for booked calls, the more firmly it keeps the call outcome on the call.
  • Credit is its own record with a role and a share, not a column per role.
  • The first source is frozen, the latest source updates, and stage changes are logged with a timestamp.

Show rate has no standard definition. iClosed defines it two different ways in its own docs. Whatever the redesign computes, write the definitions down in the repo.

6The model I recommend

Three things that are different today get separated: the person, each attempt to sell to them, and everything that happens along the way. Today the opportunity is all three.

Rules that make the model hold

  1. Every channel that brings a person in creates or finds them. Lead gen capture, Slack, Calendly and side deals all call the same identity function, so the person exists before any deal does. The DM portal creates no one: DM closers work people Slack already qualified, and the Calendly booking finds its person by the identifiers the invitee enters (decision 4, revised 20 October). Every lead gen capture becomes a person in the Prospect stage (decision 1), keyed by the Instagram identifier, and the prospect row keeps a real leadId.
  2. Identity uses strong keys only. Placeholder values never count as keys, and no time-window heuristic can join a booking to another deal. An ambiguous match goes to a review queue, the way potentialDuplicateLeadId works today.
  3. Each fact has one owner. Attendance lives on the meeting, the next step on a task, and credit in deal attributions. Projections can copy them for speed, but a projection rebuilds from the owner and never the other way round.
  4. Attribution is resolved once. When a deal is created, the CRM writes attribution rows for the lead gen worker (from the prospect), the setter (from Slack), the DM team and DM closer (from UTMs) and the closer (from the host). Reassigning a meeting adds a row and never replaces one. There are no shares yet (decision 2). When commissions arrive, each row can take a share the way Salesforce splits do: revenue credit totalling 100%, or overlay credit with no cap, frozen when the deal is won.
  5. The first source is frozen. The person keeps a first touch (who found them, through which channel) that nothing overwrites, plus a latest touch that updates. Each deal takes a snapshot of both when it is created, so later edits cannot change past commission.
  6. Each entity has one write path. One function per entity, such as transitionOpportunity(ctx, opportunityId, change), owns the state machine and domain events, and no other code patches status. Aggregates and projections that only copy data hang off convex-helpers triggers, so a new write path cannot forget them.
  7. A customer is a stage, not a table. The person reaches Customer at their first payment. Each program they buy is an enrollment with its own active, paused or churned status. The contact fields stay on the person.

Considered and not recommended (yet)

HubSpot's Leads object and iClosed's discovery calls give setter qualification its own record, with qualified and disqualified as final states, before any deal exists. That fits teams whose setters run a multi-step pipeline inside the CRM. Your setters qualify in one Slack step, and a DM closer then sends the booking link, so a deal that starts at Qualified, plus touches for the conversation, covers it with one entity fewer. A separate record would only pay off if setters ran multi-step sequences inside the CRM.

7Migration path

Revised on 14 October after the decisions in section 9, and again on 19 October after Phase 1, when the tenant admin changed how phases run. Production has one live beta tenant that accepts the risk, so every phase follows widen, migrate, narrow and ships on its own, but nothing waits for a date and nothing is left for a later phase. Each phase runs in one thread, end to end, and the thread stops when the phase is done. The rule in full is "How a phase runs" in ADR 0001.

How a phase runs (19 October)

  • One phase per thread, finished end to end. Plan, decisions, code, deploy, backfill, assert, narrow, record. The thread does not start the next phase.
  • Nothing is deferred. A phase ends when every item in its card is live and its narrow has shipped. Work that belongs to a later phase is written into that phase's card, not into a "left for later" list.
  • No waiting periods. No 24-hour recheck, no 30-day hold before a narrow. A narrow ships as soon as the backfill and assert pass and a read-only recheck agrees. Rollback is a reviewed fix-forward repair. A pre-migration export is deleted when the phase ends.
  • Data changes stay deliberate. The plan doc holds the snapshot, schema, write paths, each backfill with its assert and expected counts, deploy order, decisions and rollback. Every decision and every production run, deletions included, goes to the tenant admin in one batch. Once answered, the phase runs to the end without further approval stops.
  • Each PR deploys alone. Merging deploys to production. Additive schema first, code that tolerates unbackfilled rows, and a required-field narrow only after its assert has passed in production.

8What not to copy

  • Convert and copy. Salesforce's lead conversion splits history across records and creates duplicates when people return. Our customers table is a small version of it. Don't add another one, such as a "qualified lead" table that gets copied into a deal.
  • Appointment outcomes as deal stages. The GoHighLevel setups that add Booked, Confirmed, Showed and No-show stages, and Close's No-Show status, produce a pipeline that tracks the calendar instead of the sale. It is the same mix as our status field.
  • Lifecycle that moves backwards on its own. HubSpot blocks it for a reason. Once a person has been a customer, a lost deal should not make them a lead again in reports.
  • Crediting bookings instead of shows and closes. Practitioners and Hyros both warn that it pays setters to book anyone. Attribution rows should record the role, and future commission rules can decide what counts.
  • Overwriting the original source. Pipedrive and GoHighLevel freeze it. Our opportunity already keeps first-booking attribution, so carry that rule over to the person.
  • Dropping credit on reassignment. HubSpot removes a deal's split when the owner changes. Reassigning a meeting to another closer (Feature H) should add an attribution row, not replace one.
  • A big-bang rewrite. One live tenant with 10,800 opportunities and a reporting stack built on the current fields cannot absorb it. Every phase in section 7 runs both shapes side by side until the readers have moved.
  • Configuring everything before anyone needs it. Configurable pipelines are the right end state for a product with many tenants, but you have locked the funnel. Defining the stages once, in one module, keeps that option open for little cost.
